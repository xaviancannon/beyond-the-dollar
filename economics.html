The foundation

What Is Economics?

Economics is the study of how people and societies use limited resources to satisfy their needs and wants. Because resources are scarce, individuals, companies, and governments must make choices about what to produce, how to produce it, and who receives it.

🏠

Microeconomics

Studies individual consumers, workers, and businesses, including how they make decisions and interact in markets.

🌎

Macroeconomics

Studies the economy as a whole, including inflation, unemployment, economic growth, and national policy.

01 · Markets

Supply & Demand

Supply and demand help explain how markets determine the prices and quantities of goods and services.

📦

Supply

Supply is the quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to sell at different prices over a given period.

Example: If the price of a product rises, businesses may be willing to produce and sell more of it.

🛒

Demand

Demand is the quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to buy at different prices over a given period.

Example: If the price of a product falls, consumers may be willing to purchase more of it.

Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium occurs when the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded at a particular price. At this point, the market has no shortage or surplus at that price.

Shortage Quantity demanded is greater than quantity supplied.
Surplus Quantity supplied is greater than quantity demanded.

Real-world connection: If a popular product becomes harder to produce while consumer demand stays strong, its market price may rise.

02 · Purchasing power

Inflation

Inflation affects the purchasing power of money and influences decisions made by households and businesses.

📈

What Is Inflation?

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services over time. When prices rise, each unit of money generally buys fewer goods and services than before.

Inflation is commonly measured using price indexes, such as the Consumer Price Index (CPI), which tracks changes in the prices paid by consumers for a representative basket of goods and services.

Demand-Pull Inflation

Can occur when overall demand for goods and services grows faster than the economy's ability to supply them.

Cost-Push Inflation

Can occur when production costs, such as materials, energy, or transportation, increase and businesses raise prices in response.

Real-world connection: If the overall price of groceries increases, a household may need to spend more to buy roughly the same basket of food.

03 · Monetary policy

Interest Rates & Monetary Policy

Interest rates influence borrowing, saving, spending, and investment throughout the economy.

🏦

Understanding Interest Rates

An interest rate is the cost of borrowing money or the return earned from lending or saving it. Higher borrowing rates generally make loans more expensive, while lower rates can make borrowing less expensive.

Higher Interest Rates

  • Borrowing generally becomes more expensive.
  • Some consumers reduce large purchases.
  • Businesses may delay certain investments.
  • Saving may become more attractive.

Lower Interest Rates

  • Borrowing generally becomes less expensive.
  • Some consumers may spend more.
  • Businesses may increase investment.
  • Saving returns may be lower.

The Federal Reserve

The Federal Reserve is the central bank of the United States. It conducts monetary policy to support its goals of maximum employment and stable prices. Its decisions can influence interest rates and broader financial conditions.

Real-world connection: Changes in interest rates can affect the cost of mortgages, car loans, business financing, and other forms of borrowing.

04 · Economic performance

GDP & Unemployment

Economists use several measurements to understand economic activity and conditions in the labor market.

📊

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

GDP measures the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders during a specific period.

Economists often examine real GDP, which adjusts for changes in prices, to better understand changes in the amount of production over time.

👥

Unemployment

The unemployment rate measures the percentage of people in the labor force who are without a job, available for work, and actively looking for work.

It is one important indicator of labor market conditions, although it does not capture every difficulty people may experience finding work.

Why Do These Measurements Matter?

GDP can help economists evaluate production and economic growth. Unemployment data can provide insight into job opportunities and labor market conditions. Neither measurement tells the entire story of an economy, so economists examine them alongside other indicators.

05 · Government and the economy

Fiscal Policy

Governments can influence economic activity through taxation and spending decisions.

🏛️

What Is Fiscal Policy?

Fiscal policy refers to how a government uses spending and taxation to influence the economy. In the United States, fiscal policy decisions are made through the federal budget process and other government actions.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Generally involves increasing government spending, reducing taxes, or both, with the goal of supporting economic activity.

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Generally involves reducing government spending, increasing taxes, or both, with the goal of slowing overall demand in the economy.

Real-world connection: A government may increase spending on infrastructure projects, which can create demand for construction services, materials, and workers.

Key Economic Terms

A few important terms that help explain how economies work.

Scarcity

The condition of having limited resources to meet unlimited wants.

Opportunity Cost

The value of the next-best alternative given up when making a choice.

Equilibrium

A market condition where quantity supplied equals quantity demanded.

Inflation

A sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services.

GDP

The market value of final goods and services produced within a country during a period.

Monetary Policy

Central bank actions that influence interest rates and financial conditions.

Fiscal Policy

Government decisions about taxation and spending that influence economic activity.

Productivity

How much output is produced from a given amount of resources or labor.
